The assignment

Pick one incident from the catalogue below. There are three tracks. Each track asks for a different deliverable, but all three use the same skeleton: a timeline, a mapping to ATT&CK (and ATLAS for attacks on AI), the Pyramid of Pain, what the evidence looked like, and an honest rating of how good your sources are. The goal is not to retell the news. It is to show what a defender could have seen, what they could not, and what you would build.

  • Track A, breach detection: write and test one detection (Suricata, Zeek, Sigma or a SIEM search).
  • Track B, hunting and intelligence: write one hunt: hypothesis, data, query, validation, and the decoys that would also match.
  • Track C, attacks on AI systems: write one detection or control for an AI system’s own logs (tool calls, prompts, egress) and the guardrail that would have prevented the harm.

This replaces the old “any breach from the last 10 years” paper and the 5-minute presentation. Dec 4 is for the Case Study 3 highlights and the guest lecture. You may propose another incident (a recent one is welcome) by Wednesday, Nov 25; it must have at least two primary sources.

Template sections

Use these headings, in this order (details are in the Word template).

Bottom line up front

Two sentences a manager could read in 20 seconds: what happened and the one change to monitoring you would make on Monday morning.

1. Summary and why you chose it (5 points)

About 100 words, for a reader who has never heard of the incident.

2. Timeline (15 points)

Five to eight dated steps as a table. Note where sources disagree.

3. ATT&CK (and ATLAS) mapping and the Pyramid of Pain (20 points)

One technique per step with the ID and name, and the rung of the Pyramid of Pain at which you would detect that step, with a reason (Roddie Ch. 6–7; Demo 14).

4. What the evidence looked like (15 points)

For at least three steps: the data source (packet, flow, Zeek or server log, endpoint, cloud audit, identity, or AI tool-call log), what it records, and what it cannot see.

5. Your track deliverable (25 points)

The detection, hunt or control for your track, with the test you ran and the result. Show known-bad and known-good data and one false positive. Offline test files made with Scapy are fine; never send crafted traffic to anything but a lab host you were given.

6. How good is the intelligence? (10 points)

Three key claims, each with the source, how it was collected, its date, and your confidence (high, moderate or low) with a reason. For recent events say what is still unconfirmed (Brown and Roberts Ch. 2).

7. Lessons and one AI question (5 points)

What the incident teaches, and how an attacker using AI tools would change it, or how an AI assistant on the defender’s side would help and what you would check before trusting it.

8. Sources (5 points)

Four or more, with links and access dates, at least two of them primary (an advisory, a vendor or victim report, a court filing, a CVE or vendor bulletin). Wikipedia does not count.
